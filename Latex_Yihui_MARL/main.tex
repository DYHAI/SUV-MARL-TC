\begin{abstract}
Tropical cyclones (TCs) are destructive, while intensity and rapid-intensification
forecasts remain limited by sparse near-core observations. Platforms rarely
combine rapid transit, surface-wind sampling, and subsurface-current sensing.
We develop simulation-based parameter-sharing independent proximal policy
optimization (IPPO) for coordinated observation with Blue Whale-class
submersible unmanned vessels. Event-specific environments
align ERA5 10 m winds, HYCOM currents from 0 to 60 m, and IBTrACS tracks for a
500-TC western North Pacific archive. The surface policy uses shared winds; the
underwater policy uses each vehicle's local current history. Decisions occur
every 10 min, and the TC center enters only the training reward. Final tracking
error, the end-of-episode vessel-to-center distance on unseen TCs, is an
operational proxy for observation value. The 20 m/s policy lowers final tracking
error relative to 12 m/s in all 20 fleet-size/radius comparisons; 12 m/s yields
shorter, lower-speed paths. Low-pass filtering further lowers fleet-mean final
error, path length, and peak speed. An interim seven-depth test on 23 common
held-out TCs compares active IPPO, random control, and passive drift. Active
IPPO raises mean experienced current speed by 1.6--5.6\% and path length by
3.7--7.8 times versus passive drift. Active 2.0 m/s paths are 1.24--1.32 times
longer than 1.5 m/s paths. Results support active surface tracking and
subsurface sampling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\begin{sloppypar}
TCs threaten coastal communities, infrastructure, and
offshore operations, and the proportion of intense and rapidly intensifying
events has increased in many basins \citep{r1,r2,r3,r4}. Landfalling TCs over
the western North Pacific and China have also become more destructive
\citep{r5,r6,r7,r8}. Track prediction has advanced through numerical weather
prediction, satellite assimilation, ensembles, and data-driven models, whereas
intensity prediction remains more difficult \citep{r9,r10}. A central reason is
that intensity depends on air--sea enthalpy and momentum exchange, upper-ocean
heat content, barrier layers, mesoscale eddies, and TC-induced currents
\citep{r11,r12}. Yet clouds and rain limit microwave sensing, scatterometers
saturate at high winds, and spaceborne sensors cannot resolve the subsurface
ocean. Near-core wind and upper-ocean states therefore remain poorly constrained
in many forecast initial conditions \citep{r13,r14,r15}.
\end{sloppypar}

Existing observing platforms each cover only part of this problem. Satellites
provide broad coverage but cannot simultaneously observe the surface wind
maximum and three-dimensional upper ocean \citep{r13}; aircraft obtain
high-quality near-core measurements but are
expensive and campaign-limited \citep{r14,r15}; and gliders can remain at sea
for long periods but move much more slowly than a translating TC
\citep{r16}. Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) can adapt their sampling to
changing environmental and vehicle states \citep{r17}. Upper-ocean observations can improve initial states and, in
some cases, intensity forecasts \citep{r18,r19,r20,r21,r22}, but their value is
highly sensitive to sampling location and background structure
\citep{r23,r24,r25}. Adaptive TC observation is therefore a sequential decision
problem involving motion, sampling time, energy, and coordination rather than
coverage alone.

A submersible unmanned vessel (SUV) combines rapid surface transit with
subsurface sensing \citep{r28,r29,r30,r31}. The Blue Whale is used as an
engineering reference: its public specifications indicate a surface speed of
about 23 kn (11.83 m/s), a submerged speed of about 3 kn (1.54 m/s), and a
reference depth near 60 m \citep{r28}. Rather than claiming a digital twin, this
study parameterizes identifiable future Blue Whale-class capabilities. In the
proposed mission, the surface fleet observes 10 m winds, follows the TC toward
the high-wind region as closely as possible, and is evaluated by its final
center distance. At depth, four SUVs are assigned to each prescribed layer and
seek the strongest observable current. Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}
illustrates this handoff; four vehicles are shown at the surface as an example,
and the same parameter-sharing policy is later evaluated with 4, 8, 12, and
16 SUVs.

Classical graph search, sampling-based planning, potential fields, and model
predictive control are effective when the cost map and dynamics are known
\citep{r43,r44,r45,r46,r47,r48}. In a TC, however, the fields are nonstationary
and only locally observed, while propulsion is superimposed on ambient flow.
Conventional pipelines also estimate a field or waypoint sequence before
execution. By contrast, reinforcement learning (RL) maps each new measurement
directly to the next action, closing the observation--decision--motion loop.
RL has demonstrated strong sequential decision making across diverse domains
\citep{r49,r50,r51,r52,r53,r54,r55,r56}, and independent PPO is competitive in
cooperative multi-agent tasks \citep{r41,r42,r57}. Multi-AUV adaptive sampling
and target tracking provide the ocean-robotics foundations
\citep{r32,r33,r34,r35,r36}.

Three gaps remain. First, prior TC observation studies often represent the
ocean with analytical or reduced-order flows rather than event-specific fields
\citep{r26,r27}. Second, track-forecast errors require rapid, adaptive transit
from an uncertain deployment point to the high-wind region \citep{r23,r24,r25}.
Third, the closest multi-AUV active-observation study used only four vehicles
\citep{r27}. We therefore formulate stochastic policy optimization under partial
observability \citep{r40,r41,r42}, extend the earlier center-inference ideas
\citep{r26,r27} to event-specific surface--subsurface observation, and evaluate
4-, 8-, 12-, and 16-SUV fleets.

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We construct an event-specific learning environment for 500 TCs by
aligning ERA5 10 m winds, HYCOM currents at seven depths from 0 to 60 m, and
IBTrACS tracks.
\item Wind-only parameter-sharing IPPO learns inward motion from training-time
center rewards and lowers the final tracking error, measuring how closely the
surface fleet follows the TC and enters the high-wind region. The actor never
receives the center coordinate; during validation it infers an implicit target
direction from shared wind observations.
\item We compare active IPPO, random control, and passive drift for subsurface
observation with four SUVs at each depth, using the highest attainable observed
current speed as the objective.
\item We quantify how experienced current speed and sampling displacement vary
over seven depths from 0 to 60 m, separating vertical attenuation of the TC
response from the added mobility produced by active control.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Observation Task and Design Requirements}

The objective is to coordinate a fleet of submersible unmanned vessels
(SUVs) to observe a moving tropical cyclone (TC) across the sea surface and
upper ocean. At the surface, each SUV uses its environmental observation to move
toward the high-wind region around the moving TC; the best-track center is used
for training rewards and evaluation, not as an actor input. The subsurface
stage is then initialized within the 200 km near-core region, where four SUVs
are assigned to a prescribed depth and seek the highest attainable current
speed. The framework is designed to satisfy four operational requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
\item rapid correction of initial deployment errors using surface propulsion;
\item cooperative tracking of the moving TC through decentralized action
selection, using onboard wind observations and idealized inter-vehicle sharing;
\item subsurface observation at ocean depths of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and
60 m below the sea surface;
\item safe and controllable operation under the specified energy,
communication, smoothing, and coastline settings.
\end{enumerate}

The surface maximum speed is varied between 12 and 20 m/s, while the submerged
maximum propulsion speed is 1.5 or 2.0 m/s. This difference creates an
asymmetric decision problem: surface propulsion is used to follow the TC and
reach the high-wind region as closely as possible, quantified primarily by the
final tracking error. At depth, the objective changes to finding and sampling
the highest possible current speed, so the four SUVs must weigh active
propulsion against passive advection by the ambient current.

\subsection{Problem Definition}

The fleet contains $N$ SUVs, where $N\in\{4,8,12,16\}$, indexed by
$i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}$. The position, propulsion velocity,
and operating mode of SUV $i$ at time $t$ are denoted by
$\mathbf{x}_i(t)$, $\mathbf{p}_i(t)$, and $m_i(t)\in\{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{u}\}$,
respectively, where $\mathrm{s}$ and $\mathrm{u}$ denote the surface and
subsurface modes. The propulsion speed satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf{p}_i(t)\|\leq
\begin{cases}
V_{\max}^{\mathrm{s}}, & m_i(t)=\mathrm{s},\\
V_{\max}^{\mathrm{u}}, & m_i(t)=\mathrm{u}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:speed_constraint}
\end{equation}

Each simulated episode is assigned to one stage, consistent with the separate
surface and subsurface environments used in training and evaluation. Thus,
$m_i(t)$ selects the applicable speed bound and motion law for that episode;
the handoff from surface transit to subsurface sampling is represented by the
stage initialization, whereas continuous diving, ballast transitions, and
resurfacing are not resolved as controlled degrees of freedom.

The horizontal motion of the SUV is modeled as
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_i(t)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{p}_i(t), & m_i(t)=\mathrm{s},\\[1mm]
\mathbf{p}_i(t)+
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ocean}}
\left(\mathbf{x}_i(t),z_i,t\right), & m_i(t)=\mathrm{u},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:platform_motion}
\end{equation}
where $z_i\in\{0,10,20,30,40,50,60\}$ m and
$\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ocean}}(\cdot)$ is the HYCOM current at the corresponding
position and depth. Thus, the underwater ground velocity is the superposition of
active propulsion and background advection.

For numerical implementation, the continuous model is integrated explicitly at
the decision interval $\Delta t=10$ min:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{i,k+1}=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{x}_{i,k}+\mathbf{p}_{i,k}\Delta t,
& m_{i,k}=\mathrm{s},\\[1mm]
\mathbf{x}_{i,k}+
\left[
\mathbf{p}_{i,k}+
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ocean}}(\mathbf{x}_{i,k},z_i,t_k)
\right]\Delta t,
& m_{i,k}=\mathrm{u}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:platform_motion_discrete}
\end{equation}
The current field is evaluated at the beginning of each interval and
interpolated to the current vehicle position.
For the active underwater reward, we write this pre-move current as
$\mathbf{u}_{i,k}=\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ocean}}
(\mathbf{x}_{i,k},z_i,t_k)$.

This equation is deliberately a reduced-order horizontal point-mass
kinematic--advection model rather than a six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body
dynamic model. It does not resolve added mass, hydrodynamic drag, roll, pitch,
yaw or heading dynamics, buoyancy and ballast dynamics, thruster transients,
wave loads, or actuator delay. This simplification is consistent with the
information content of the environmental forcing. ERA5 winds are available at
$0.25^\circ$ and hourly resolution, whereas HYCOM currents are available at
approximately $0.04^\circ$ by $0.08^\circ$ and three-hourly resolution. These
fields are interpolated to the 10 min decision interval and to each vehicle
position, but such interpolation does not recover high-frequency wave forcing,
local wake effects, or unobserved vehicle attitude and actuator states.
Introducing a detailed six-degree-of-freedom model would therefore require
platform-specific hydrodynamic coefficients and forcing information that are
not commensurate with the present data, and could imply a level of numerical
precision unsupported by the inputs. This choice reflects the mission-level
scope and available calibration data, not a claim that six-degree-of-freedom
effects are operationally negligible. The reduced model retains the first-order
effects relevant to mission-level planning: mode-dependent speed limits,
propulsion commands, ambient-current advection in subsurface mode, and coastal
constraints. Accordingly, the results are interpreted as mission-planning and
coordination evidence rather than as predictions of attitude, structural
loading, or low-level controller response. Coupling this planning layer to an
experimentally calibrated six-degree-of-freedom vehicle model is left for future work.

The local observation of SUV $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{o}_i(t)=
\left[
\mathbf{s}_i(t),
\mathbf{v}_i(t),
\mathbf{h}_i(t)
\right],
\label{eq:observation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{s}_i(t)$ contains the observation used in the corresponding
stage and $\mathbf{v}_i(t)$ is the normalized commanded propulsion velocity,
$\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{p}_i/V_{\max}^{m_i}$; it is not the total ground velocity.
In the final surface experiments, $\mathbf{s}_i(t)$ consists only of the
normalized wind patches at 10 m above the sea surface sampled by the $N$ SUVs,
and no historical frame is used. Surface operation assumes idealized, lossless,
and instantaneous inter-vehicle communication: every SUV receives the wind observations acquired
by all other SUVs in the fleet as part of its policy input. In the underwater
active-control comparison, no inter-vehicle communication is assumed.
Accordingly, $\mathbf{s}_i(t)$ consists only of SUV $i$'s local current speed
and components at its assigned ocean depth, while $\mathbf{h}_i(t)$ contains a
five-frame history of that same vehicle's local current. Each underwater SUV
therefore knows neither the current observations nor the positions or actions
of the other SUVs. The complete reanalysis field, the global best-track center,
and future environmental states are not provided to the policy.
The final surface input also excludes agent-identity encodings and prior-frame
history; agents are differentiated only by their current positions, wind
measurements, and propulsion velocities.
Accordingly, the surface observation dimension is $d_o^{\mathrm{s}}=3N+2$.
The underwater active controller uses a $1\times1$ local-current observation
plus a five-frame history and two normalized propulsion components, giving
$d_o^{\mathrm{u}}=5\times3+2=17$.

During training, the reward function uses the distance between each SUV and the
best-track TC center to shape an association between local 10 m wind patterns
and movement toward the center/high-wind region. This is reward-based policy
learning, not supervised learning from center labels, and the center coordinate
itself is not an actor input. During validation, rewards do not influence
actions. The frozen policy receives only the shared fleet-level wind samples and
must move toward an unseen TC, thereby testing whether the learned
wind-to-inward-motion behavior transfers across events.

The normalized action is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_i(t)\in[-1,1]^2,
\end{equation}
and is projected onto the unit disk before being mapped to propulsion in both
modes:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_i(t)=
\frac{\mathbf{a}_i(t)}
{\max\left(1,\|\mathbf{a}_i(t)\|_2\right)},\qquad
\mathbf{p}_i(t)=
V_{\max}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_i(t).
\label{eq:normalized_action}
\end{equation}
This mapping ensures that the total propulsion speed never exceeds the
mode-specific speed limit.

The cooperative task is a decentralized partially observable Markov decision
process (Dec-POMDP):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}=
\left\langle
\mathcal{I},
\mathcal{S},
\{\mathcal{O}_i\},
\{\mathcal{A}_i\},
P,
R,
\gamma
\right\rangle,
\label{eq:decpomdp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of SUVs, $\mathcal{S}$ is the joint environment
state, $\mathcal{O}_i$ and $\mathcal{A}_i$ are the local observation and action
spaces of vehicle $i$, $P$ is the transition probability, $r_i$ is the local
reward contribution, $R=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_i$ is the shared team reward, and
$\gamma$ is the discount factor.

\section{Data and Simulation Environment}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Reanalysis and Best-Track Products}

ERA5 (ECMWF Reanalysis v5) supplies hourly zonal and meridional wind components
at 10 m above the sea surface on a regular $0.25^\circ$ grid
\footnote{ERA5 download portal:
\url{https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-single-levels}.}
\citep{r37}. Wind speed is calculated from $u_{10}$ and $v_{10}$; the policy
uses the speed together with the two velocity components. HYCOM (Hybrid
Coordinate Ocean Model) supplies three-hourly zonal and meridional
currents at ocean depths of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 m below the sea
surface
\footnote{HYCOM GLBy0.08 archive:
\url{https://www.hycom.org/data/glby0p08/expt-93pt0}; HYCOM ESPC-D-V02 archive:
\url{https://www.hycom.org/data/espcd-v02}.}
\citep{r38}. The native horizontal grid is
approximately $0.04^\circ$ in latitude by $0.08^\circ$ in longitude. IBTrACS
(International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship) provides TC time,
center position, maximum wind, minimum pressure, and related metadata at mostly
3 or 6 h intervals
\footnote{IBTrACS download portal:
\url{https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/international-best-track-archive}.}
\citep{r39}. The common study domain is
$98.7^\circ$E--$171.6^\circ$E and $6.8^\circ$N--$56.7^\circ$N. The overall
event set spans 19 November 2007 to 3 December 2025 UTC. Thus, the surface
observation variable is the 10 m wind velocity and speed, whereas the
subsurface observation variables are the horizontal current velocity,
represented by \texttt{water\_u} and \texttt{water\_v}, and the derived current
speed at the seven sampled depths between 0 and 60 m.

temporal resolutions used to construct the event-specific environments.

\subsection{Spatiotemporal Alignment and Track Segmentation}

All fields are mapped to a common simulation clock. Surface atmospheric fields
are linearly interpolated from hourly values, and ocean currents are linearly
interpolated from three-hourly values to the 10 min decision interval.
This is an offline reanalysis simulation: adjacent reanalysis samples are used
to reconstruct the field at the simulated time, and the policy receives only
the interpolated local value at that time, not a future time index or full
future field. This construction does not emulate the data latency of a
real-time forecast system. At each decision time, the observation operator
uses the reconstructed field, while the best-track center is used only for
training supervision and evaluation. Any
NaN or infinity produced by interpolation or coastal masking is replaced by a
pre-specified neutral value after finite-difference gradients are computed. The
resulting observations are checked for finiteness before entering the policy.

Although the native grids and sampling intervals are much coarser than the
10 min decision step, the environment performs spatial and temporal
interpolation on demand at every decision time. Each SUV queries the interpolated field at
its current position and at the corresponding simulation time, and the same
interpolated values are used for its observation and for current-induced
motion. These values are treated as the best available estimates from the
archive under the stated interpolation scheme, rather than as resolved
high-resolution truth or statistically optimal reconstructions of unobserved
sub-grid variability.

Policy training and evaluation are offline with respect to the environmental
data: the ERA5 and HYCOM trajectories are precomputed, and vehicle motion does
not feed back into the wind or current fields. This one-way forcing assumption
is motivated by the large separation of scales between a small SUV and a
TC-scale atmosphere--ocean system, for which the momentum and energy transferred
by the vehicles are negligible relative to those driving the environmental
fields. It differs from an online two-way coupled simulation in which actions
modify the environment and the modified environment then changes subsequent
observations. The term offline here does not mean batch or offline RL: the
policy is still trained through sequential closed-loop interaction with the
precomputed environment, but the environmental trajectory itself is independent
of the policy. A two-way coupled simulation would be required if vehicle-induced
wakes or other local environmental feedback were the object of study.

The 10 min decision interval is selected as a compromise between control
responsiveness and the information content of the source products. A longer
interval would reduce the frequency of trajectory correction and degrade
decision precision. A substantially shorter interval would increase decision
and execution overhead and would substantially increase the number of
decisions per episode, the required interaction samples, and the training
workload. It would also require temporal detail beyond what can be supported by
interpolating hourly ERA5 winds and three-hourly HYCOM currents. Using such an
interval would therefore create a false impression of higher temporal
resolution without introducing genuinely new environmental information.

Each IBTrACS track is first linearly interpolated to the 10 min simulation
interval. The cumulative along-track distance is computed using the local
equirectangular approximation
\begin{equation}
\Delta s_k =
\sqrt{
\left[
111\Delta\lambda_k\cos(\varphi_k)
\right]^2
+
\left(111\Delta\varphi_k\right)^2
},
\label{eq:segment_distance}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\lambda_k$ and $\Delta\varphi_k$ are the longitudinal and
latitudinal increments in degrees. The constant 111 km is the mean meridional
arc length per degree, and $\cos(\varphi_k)$ converts a longitudinal degree to
the corresponding zonal distance at latitude $\varphi_k$. The target length of
each segment is sampled
uniformly from 300--500 km. The start of the next segment is shifted by a fixed
250 km along the track, producing an overlap of 50--250 km and an average
overlap of approximately 150 km. A new segment is initialized only when at
least 300 km of track remains; the final residual is discarded. Because segment
endpoints are assigned to the nearest 10 min interpolation point, the realized
segment lengths are 292.1--507.1 km (median 396.4 km).

The 500-TC archive yields 7,248 segments from 495 TCs with sufficient track
length. The training split contains 450 TCs, of which 445 contribute at least
one usable segment, yielding 6,480 training segments. The held-out test split
contains 50 TCs and 768 segments. The training and test sets are separated at
the TC level, so segments from the same TC never appear in both sets. Segment
durations range from 2.2 to 103 h, with median and mean durations of 16.5 and
20.0 h, respectively.

Track segmentation is not merely a data-processing choice; it reflects the
physical operating limit of the surface platform. The surface endurance of an
SUV is finite, so one deployment cannot observe the complete life cycle of a
TC. A 300--500 km along-track segment instead represents a self-contained
mission in which the fleet can correct forecast-induced displacement, reach a
physically relevant part of the TC, and collect a sustained observation
sequence representative of a bounded surface mission. This segment length is the
distance traveled by the TC along its track, not a cap on the total
vehicle-route length; turning, lateral correction, and search motion can make
an individual SUV path longer. The simulation uses path length as a propulsion
cost proxy and does not impose a detailed battery state-of-charge model. After
entering the near-core region, the vehicles can, in the mission concept, dive
and sample the upper ocean at several depths. The Blue Whale-class platform
provides the surface-transit scale and reference depth used in the present
model. A future reconfigurable payload could support current profilers, CTD,
and biogeochemical sensors, so the observing concept could collect temperature,
salinity, current velocity, nutrients, oxygen, and related variables. The
control experiments deliberately use current velocity only as the
decision-making observation, avoiding model dependence on variables that are
not consistently available across the complete 500-TC archive. This
restriction does not imply that the physical platform would observe only
currents.

\subsection{Blue Whale-Class Platform Modeling}

\subsection{Decentralized Partially Observable Decision Process}

The cooperative observation task uses the Dec-POMDP defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decpomdp}. In that formulation, the surface input concatenates
the $3N$ normalized wind-observation values acquired across the fleet with the
vehicle's own two normalized velocity components; it is memoryless and relies
on idealized inter-vehicle communication. In the active underwater setting,
each vehicle instead uses only its own current observation, causal five-frame
current history, and propulsion velocity. Shared policy parameters therefore do
not imply shared observations, states, or actions. In both stages, the actor
receives neither the complete environmental field, the global best-track
center, nor future reanalysis frames; the best track is used only for training
rewards and evaluation metrics.

The policy operates as a closed perception--decision--action loop rather than
following a precomputed static route. At every 10 min decision interval, it
observes the current environment, generates a new action, moves the vehicle,
observes the updated environment, and repeats:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{o}_{i,t}
&\longrightarrow
\mathbf{a}_{i,t}=\pi_\theta(\mathbf{o}_{i,t})\\
&\longrightarrow
\mathbf{x}_{i,t+1}
\longrightarrow
\mathbf{o}_{i,t+1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:closed_loop}
\end{equation}
This feedback process allows the fleet to revise its motion as the wind and
current fields evolve. The observation is not only an output recorded after
motion; it is also the next control input that determines subsequent motion.
Here, "supervision" refers to this measurement-driven feedback role rather than
to supervised learning with target action labels: the policy is trained from
scalar rewards, but each new observation directly conditions the next decision
through the closed-loop state. Because the environment is nonstationary and
stochastic, observations affect subsequent motion continuously rather than
being used only to construct a map or waypoint sequence before execution. This
distinction applies specifically to planning pipelines that produce and then
execute a static route; it does not imply that all model-predictive or adaptive
planners are open loop. The same mechanism supports adaptation to changing
local observations, partial observability, and deployment uncertainty.

\subsection{Surface and Subsurface Reward Design}

The surface objective is to follow the translating TC as closely as possible
and reach the high-wind region. Its primary operational metric is the final
tracking error, defined as the fleet average of the individual SUV-to-center
distances at the final step. Let $d_i$ denote the distance from SUV $i$ to the
TC center. The surface reward used in the implementation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_i^{\mathrm{surf}}={}&
-\frac{\min(d_i,300)}{100}
+b_{\mathrm{near}}\mathbf{1}_{d_i<100}\\
&-\frac{W_{\mathrm{form}}
\max(0,d_i-R_{\mathrm{form}})}{100}\\
&-\lambda_u\|\mathbf{a}_{i,t}-\mathbf{a}_{i,t-1}\|_2^2
+r_i^{\mathrm{coast}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:surface_reward}
\end{equation}
where the first term is a bounded proximity cost and the second term provides
a discrete near-center bonus with $b_{\mathrm{near}}=1$. The third term adds an
additional radius-excess penalty beyond $R_{\mathrm{form}}=100$ km, and the
fourth term penalizes abrupt action changes with $\lambda_u=0.05$. The term
$r_i^{\mathrm{coast}}$ represents the coastal-safety reward defined below. The
300 km cap prevents distant errors from producing an excessively steep
gradient and reduces premature policy collapse during early training. After
aggregation over SUVs, the team average reward is clipped to $[-5,2]$.

For subsurface observation, four SUVs are assigned to each prescribed depth.
Their objective is not to follow the surface TC center directly, but to observe
the highest possible current speed. Low-speed SUVs cannot generally overtake a
translating TC, and the vertical projection of the surface center is not
necessarily the location of the strongest subsurface current. For the
active-control comparison, the current-seeking reward is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_i^{\mathrm{sub}}={}&
\min\left(
\frac{\|\mathbf{u}_{i,k}\|}{V_{\mathrm{norm}}},
R_{\max}
\right)\\
&-\lambda_E\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_i\|_2^2
+\beta_{\parallel}\max(0,\cos\theta_i)\\
&+r_i^{\mathrm{coast}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sub_reward}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{norm}}=1.0$ m/s, $R_{\max}=2.0$,
$\lambda_E=0.05$, and $\beta_{\parallel}=0.5$. The angle $\theta_i$ denotes
the direction difference between propulsion and the pre-move current
$\mathbf{u}_{i,k}$, so the positive-alignment term is activated only when the
SUV pushes downstream; formally,
$\cos\theta_i=
(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_i\cdot\mathbf{u}_{i,k})/
(\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_i\|_2\|\mathbf{u}_{i,k}\|_2)$
when the denominator is nonzero. The current-intensity reward is therefore
evaluated at $\mathbf{x}_{i,k}$ before the state update.
For the local maximum-current objective, passive drift is retained as the
no-propulsion reference because it follows the ambient flow directly and does
not depend on a learned reward policy.

\subsection{Coastal Safety and Action Smoothing}

The land mask is converted into a nonnegative offshore-distance field. A vehicle
inside land receives a large penalty, and a vehicle within the buffer distance
$D_{\mathrm{buf}}$ receives
\begin{equation}
r_i^{\mathrm{coast}}=
\begin{cases}
-P_{\mathrm{land}}, & D_i=0,\\
-W_b\max(0,1-D_i/D_{\mathrm{buf}}), & 0<D_i<D_{\mathrm{buf}},\\
0, & D_i\geq D_{\mathrm{buf}},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:coast}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is the offshore distance and $P_{\mathrm{land}}$ and $W_b$ are
positive penalty coefficients.

Three complementary mechanisms are used to reduce coastline intrusion:
offshore-only rejection sampling during deployment, a soft nearshore penalty,
and an action-level safety filter. The current main experiments use a minimum
deployment distance of 100 km, a 100 km nearshore buffer, and a 100 km safety
margin. The deployment step removes invalid initial states, the reward
discourages trajectories near land, and the safety filter removes the onshore
action component when an SUV approaches the shoreline. These mechanisms reduce
land exposure but are not posed as an unconditional hard constraint on every
vehicle position.

To reduce oscillatory control, the main surface experiments use an exponential
action filter:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{filt}}_t=
(1-\beta)\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t+
\beta\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{filt}}_{t-1},
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
where $\beta\in[0,1)$ controls the degree of temporal smoothing. The filter
uses only the current and previous actions and therefore does not introduce
future-information leakage.

\subsection{Parameter-Sharing Independent PPO}

All SUVs contribute to the same observation and tracking objective, and the
training return is defined as the team-average reward. The problem is therefore
a fully cooperative task, rather than a competitive or mixed
cooperative--competitive task. This cooperative reward structure motivates the
use of IPPO, in which decentralized vehicle actions are optimized against a
common team objective while policy parameters are shared across vehicles.

In this paper, IPPO denotes a cooperative, parameter-sharing implementation of
independent PPO. All SUVs use the same actor and critic weights, while their
observations and action samples remain vehicle-specific. Actions are sampled
independently, and the per-agent values are summed for training without a
global-state centralized critic. The main surface model uses a memoryless
multilayer perceptron, whereas the active underwater controller uses a
five-frame current history. The shared cooperative return is the team-average
reward, and a single clipped PPO objective is optimized over the factorized
multi-agent action vector:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)
={}&\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[
\min\Bigl(
\rho_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\\
&\qquad
\operatorname{clip}
\left(\rho_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)
\hat A_t
\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ppo}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\rho_t(\theta)=
\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid o_{i,t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{i,t}\mid o_{i,t})},
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
and $\hat A_t$ is estimated by generalized advantage estimation from the
team-average reward. The actor objective is complemented by a clipped value
loss and an entropy bonus. During execution, each SUV computes its action from
its own observation and the shared policy network.

Off-policy SAC and TD3 were not used for the final multi-SUV comparison. Both
require a replay buffer, and the storage per transition increases with the joint
observation and action dimensions of up to 16 vehicles. Large-scale parallel
sampling also fills replay memory quickly and adds replay-management overhead.
The final experiments instead prioritize stable parallel on-policy sampling and
use parameter-sharing IPPO. This is an implementation and resource constraint,
not a claim that IPPO is universally more sample-efficient than SAC or TD3.

The complete reward coefficients, safety parameters, network dimensions, and
PPO hyperparameters are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:parameters}.
The project repository and software package sources are listed in the Code and
package availability section.

\subsection{Surface Evaluation and Generalization}
\label{subsec:surface_protocol}

The center-distance reward is available only during training. It does not enter
the actor observation and is absent during validation. Consequently, the
surface results test a specific form of generalization: a frozen policy must
infer where to move from shared wind observations in an unseen TC. The policy
is not asked to read the best-track center, and it cannot exploit a reward
computed from that center. This distinction is essential for interpreting the
100--800 km curves as forecast-error tolerance rather than as supervised center
tracking.

Operationally, surface observation benefit is represented by the final tracking
error (km), the fleet-averaged SUV-to-best-track-center distance at the final
time step. A smaller value denotes a higher benefit because the fleet ends
closer to the TC core. Time-averaged distance is retained as a secondary
process diagnostic and is computed analogously, with the final-time distance in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:final_tracking_error} replaced by the mean distance over all
decision steps of each episode.

For training seed \(s\) and its set of \(M_s=768\) held-out segments, the
seed-level value is
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{final}}^{(s)}
=
\frac{1}{M_s}
\sum_{e=1}^{M_s}
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left\|
\mathbf{x}_{i,e}(T_e)
-
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{TC},e}(T_e)
\right\|_2,
\label{eq:final_tracking_error}
\end{equation}
where \(T_e\) is the final decision time of test segment \(e\). The reported
final tracking error is the mean of \(D_{\mathrm{final}}^{(s)}\) over the ten
independent training seeds, with uncertainty expressed as the sample standard
deviation across seeds.

The final surface matrix uses only the wind observations collected by the fleet
and evaluates
4-, 8-, 12-, and 16-SUV formations. The 100 km ring and equal angular spacing
of \(360^\circ/N\) define the initial deployment geometry; the formation is not
constrained to remain rigid during tracking. During training, the
formation-center offset is sampled uniformly inside an 800 km disk around the
initial TC center, representing the assumed upper bound of track-forecast
position error. The trained policies are then evaluated without retraining at
fixed formation-center offsets of 100, 200, 400, 600, and 800 km with random
bearings. Because the training distribution already contains radii from 0 to
800 km, the 200 km validation case is covered without a separate 200 km-trained
model. Training at only the shortest radius would also remove the deployment
variation needed to test transfer across forecast-error levels.

For each fleet-size, deployment-radius, and speed setting, the 768 held-out
segments were first averaged within each training seed. Means and sample
standard deviations were then computed across the ten independent seeds.
Paired comparisons between 12 and 20 m/s used two-sided paired $t$-tests on the
ten seed-level values. Because the twenty fleet-size/radius comparisons were
tested separately without multiplicity correction, the resulting
$p$-values are interpreted as exploratory evidence rather than confirmatory
hypothesis tests.

\subsection{Underwater Evaluation Protocol and Baselines}
\label{subsec:underwater_protocol}

Once the high-wind region is reached, diving provides the second part of the
sampling objective. The subsurface current there is expected to contain the
upper-ocean response generated by the TC forcing and its preceding passage,
including near-inertial and mesoscale variability \citep{r11}. Sampling that dynamically
conditioned region is expected to be more informative than descending in an
unrelated background ocean. The present study evaluates the observation task
itself; it does not quantify the subsequent impact of these measurements in a
data-assimilation or forecast system.

The final subsurface matrix evaluates one four-SUV fleet at each ocean depth;
the seven depth cases are separate experiments rather than a 28-vehicle fleet.
Each episode has a nominal 20 h duration and terminates
earlier when the available track window or pre-landfall interval ends. Its
objective is to observe the highest attainable local current speed at depths
of 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 m under maximum propulsion speeds of 1.5 and
2.0 m/s. It compares active IPPO, random control, and passive drift under
otherwise identical conditions. Because the surface
stage is responsible for approaching the TC and high-wind region, the
subsurface rollout starts from the surface endpoint and initializes the four
vehicles within 200 km of the TC center. The 100--800 km forecast-error
protocol applies only to the surface stage.

For each depth and speed setting, one central-index segment is selected from
each held-out TC and evaluated under ten paired deployment seeds. The complete
50-TC test split therefore provides 500 matched episodes per policy; the current
interim test uses 23 TCs and 230 episodes.

Subsurface execution follows the local-only observation contract defined in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}. The policy parameters are shared, but no observations,
positions, or actions are exchanged between vehicles during execution.
Increasing the fleet at one depth would increase aggregate sampling and change
the total observation effort. Because no observations are exchanged, however,
additional vehicles would not provide extra information to an individual
policy; the resulting comparison would confound strategy with sampling scale.
The experiment therefore fixes the fleet at four vehicles per depth and pairs
all methods on the same episodes and deployment seeds. In a larger deployment,
four vehicles can be assigned to each of several depths, while the marginal
value and redundancy of additional vehicles per depth remain a separate design
question.

Let \(\Pi_{\mathcal D}(\mathbf a)=\mathbf a/
\max(1,\|\mathbf a\|_2)\) denote projection onto the unit disk. The three
strategies differ only in how the propulsion command is generated:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a_i^{\mathrm{active}}
&=
\Pi_{\mathcal D}
\left(\pi_\theta(\mathbf o_i)\right),\\
\mathbf a_i^{\mathrm{random}}
&=
\Pi_{\mathcal D}
\left(\mathbf e_i\right),\qquad
\mathbf e_i\sim\mathcal U\left([-1,1]^2\right),\\
\mathbf a_i^{\mathrm{drift}}
&=
\mathbf 0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:underwater_policy_actions}
\end{equation}
During evaluation, the active IPPO policy uses its deterministic mean action,
whereas random control resamples a two-dimensional action every 10 min. Both
commands are passed through the same unit-disk projection before multiplication
by \(V_{\max}^{\mathrm u}\).
Active IPPO and random control share the same propulsion limit and equations
of motion; only the active policy conditions its action on the local current
history. Passive drift applies zero propulsion and advances solely by ambient
current advection.

For vehicle \(i\) at depth \(z\), the ground-path length after \(K\) decision
steps and the mean experienced current speed are
\begin{equation}
L_i(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i,k+1}-\mathbf{x}_{i,k}\right\|_2,
\label{eq:underwater_path}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\bar V_i(z)=\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}
\left\|\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ocean}}
(\mathbf{x}_{i,k},z,t_k)\right\|_2.
\label{eq:experienced_current}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C(z)=A_{\mathrm{cell}}
\left|
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\bigcup_{k=0}^{K}
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}_{i,k})
\right|,
\label{eq:underwater_coverage}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x})\) is the \(0.05^\circ\) grid cell containing
position \(\mathbf{x}\), \(|\cdot|\) counts unique cells, and
\(A_{\mathrm{cell}}\) is the cell area in km\(^2\).
The figures report the fleet means
\(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L_i(z)\) and
\(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar V_i(z)\), while \(C(z)\) measures the union of
spatial cells sampled by the fleet.

reinforce each other: longer travel time increases the consequence of even a
small directional error, while the weaker remote wind signature makes such an
error more likely.

\subsection{Execution-Layer Action Filtering}

Table~\ref{tab:surface_control_ablations} reports the low-pass filtering
ablation over the same trained actor, 768 held-out segments, and 10 min
decision interval. Without filtering, the 4-, 8-, 12-, and 16-SUV
configurations travel 1294.9--1331.5 km per vehicle, with mean speeds of
19.23--19.73 m/s and maximum speeds of 19.92--20.00 m/s. With the
execution-layer filter ($\beta=0.8$), path length falls to 690.8--769.8 km,
mean speed to 11.11--12.72 m/s, and maximum speed to 16.13--18.20 m/s.
The final tracking error changes from 288.2 to 258.2 km for four SUVs, from
305.2 to 308.6 km for eight SUVs, from 478.5 to 330.0 km for 12 SUVs, and from
313.6 to 273.4 km for 16 SUVs. Averaged over the four fleet sizes, it decreases
from 346.4 to 292.6 km, a reduction of 53.8 km
(15.5\%). Thus, filtering not only avoids degrading final tracking but improves
it on average: three fleet sizes improve, whereas the eight-SUV case increases
only slightly, by 3.4 km.

The same comparison shows reductions for every fleet size: path length
decreases by 563.4, 547.1, 618.5, and 573.6 km for 4, 8, 12, and 16 SUVs,
respectively, while maximum speed decreases by 1.80, 2.29, 3.87, and 2.94 m/s.
The 12-SUV configuration has the largest relative path-length reduction,
although the effect is not monotonic in fleet size. These results show that the
execution filter improves final tracking on average while substantially
shortening trajectories and reducing mean and peak speed.

\subsection{Speed-Limit and Deployment-Error Analysis}

The 12 and 20 m/s cases answer different engineering questions. The 12 m/s
value is close to the published surface-speed scale of the current Blue Whale,
whereas 20 m/s represents a future high-speed scenario. A simple direct-pursuit
estimate illustrates why deployment radius changes the effect of this choice.
If the TC translates at speed \(U_{\mathrm{c}}\) and the SUV moves toward it at
\(V_{\max}\), the nominal closure rate is approximately
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{close}}=V_{\max}-U_{\mathrm{c}}.
\label{eq:closure_speed}
\end{equation}
For a deployment error \(D\), the corresponding correction time is
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{corr}}\approx\frac{D}{V_{\mathrm{close}}}.
\label{eq:correction_time}
\end{equation}
This is only a kinematic lower bound because it neglects turning and imperfect
center estimation, but it exposes the dominant scale. At 100--200 km, both
speed limits provide enough closure to correct a moderate forecast
displacement. At 600--800 km, a TC translation speed of 4--7 m/s removes a
substantial fraction of the 12 m/s speed budget. The required correction time
can then approach or exceed the 20 h mean segment duration. A 20 m/s vehicle
retains a larger reserve for reducing initial center error, but its benefit is
expected mainly at the larger radii; at short radii both cases already have
sufficient speed.

The growing 12/20 m/s final-tracking-error gap follows this closure-rate scaling:
both speeds are adequate at 100--200 km, whereas the available reserve of the
12 m/s platform contracts at 400--800 km. The 800 km test is therefore
conservative for the lower-speed platform. Operationally, large forecast errors
favor a faster vehicle, a tighter forecast-error envelope, or earlier
deployment; 12 m/s remains preferable only when shorter paths and lower
propulsion use are prioritized.

Figure~\ref{fig:underwater_methods_10seed} reports an interim seven-depth
comparison. To keep the depth comparison internally consistent, all policies
are evaluated on the same 23 held-out TCs. The 40 and 60 m active policies were
trained with the currently available archives (200 and 203 training TCs),
whereas the remaining depths use the complete training partition. The plotted
values summarize ten independently trained active policies per depth and speed
setting. At all seven depths and both propulsion limits, active IPPO attains a
higher mean experienced current speed than passive drift and random control.
The improvement over passive drift ranges from 1.6\% to 5.6\%, indicating that
the learned policy spends more of each episode in relatively high-current
water. Random control remains close to passive drift in mean current speed but
travels substantially farther.

The depth dependence of the current metric is consistent across all three
strategies. From 0 to 60 m, the mean experienced current speed decreases
monotonically from 0.526 to 0.273 m/s for the 2.0 m/s active policy (48.1\%),
from 0.514 to 0.276 m/s for the 1.5 m/s active policy (46.3\%), and from 0.504
to 0.269 m/s for passive drift (46.6\%). The similar absolute decay under
trained, random, and unpropelled motion indicates that this trend is primarily
an environmental signature rather than a policy artifact. It is consistent
with the upper-ocean response to TC forcing: wind stress transfers momentum
most strongly in the surface mixed layer, after which vertical momentum flux,
shear instability, turbulent dissipation, and mixing reduce the horizontal
current amplitude with depth \citep{r11}. The finite currents at 50--60 m also
show that the response persists throughout the sampled layer rather than
vanishing abruptly.

The longer trajectories of active IPPO represent a sampling trade-off rather
than an unconditional gain. Because all strategies sample at the same 10 min
interval, path length does not by itself increase the number of measurements
per SUV. It does, however, disperse those measurements over a larger spatial
footprint and reduce repeated sampling of nearly the same water mass. The
active path is 3.7--7.8 times that of passive drift, and the corresponding
coverage increases by roughly 3.3--6.2 times. Active control must therefore be
assessed against both objectives: a modest increase in mean experienced
current speed and broader spatial context, offset by greater propulsion use.
Passive drift remains the low-motion reference and cannot deliberately relocate
toward higher-current regions.

Path length follows the same environmental attenuation only weakly. Passive
drift has no propulsion term, so its path contracts by 50.8\% from 0 to 60 m,
almost matching the 46.6\% decrease in its experienced current. Active paths
decrease by only 17.7\% and 19.3\% at 2.0 and 1.5 m/s, respectively, because
the commanded propulsion component is independent of depth and dominates the
2.0 m/s case. Consequently, the active-to-drift path ratio grows from 4.69 to
7.84 at 2.0 m/s and from 3.69 to 6.06 at 1.5 m/s. This increase should not be
interpreted as the policy becoming progressively stronger with depth: it also
reflects the faster shrinkage of the passive-drift denominator. The small
absolute current advantage remains positive at every depth (1.6--5.6\%), while
the absolute current itself declines. Thus, upper-ocean current attenuation
sets the background depth trend, and active propulsion mainly preserves
mobility and sampling extent as that background weakens.

The propulsion limit consistently affects trajectory extent. At all seven
depths, the 2.0 m/s policy travels 1.24--1.32 times farther than the 1.5 m/s
policy because the larger speed envelope permits more movement within the same
nominal 20 h mission. This result links future platform speed to potential
sampling capacity: a faster SUV can cover more of the TC-induced current field
and place successive samples farther apart, although the associated energy and
control constraints must still be considered. The mean experienced current is
slightly lower at 2.0 m/s than at 1.5 m/s at 30 and 60 m, however. A larger
speed envelope therefore enlarges the sampling footprint but does not guarantee
a higher mean current exposure.

\section{Conclusion}

This study presented a parameter-sharing IPPO framework for coordinated surface
and subsurface TC observation with Blue Whale-class SUVs. Event-specific
environments aligned ERA5 10 m winds, HYCOM currents at seven depths from 0 to
60 m, and IBTrACS tracks for a 500-TC archive. Training and testing were
separated at the TC level to estimate transfer to unseen events.

At the surface, the 20 m/s policy produced lower final tracking error than
12 m/s in all 20 fleet-size/radius comparisons, and its advantage increased
with deployment error. The 12 m/s policy instead yielded shorter, lower-speed
trajectories. Low-pass filtering further reduced fleet-mean final tracking
error and peak speed, showing that wind-only observations support closed-loop
surface correction without direct access to the TC center.

In the interim seven-depth comparison, active IPPO increased mean experienced
current speed by 1.6--5.6\%, path length by 3.7--7.8 times, and sampled area by
3.3--6.2 times relative to passive drift. The longer paths represent broader,
less redundant spatial sampling at the cost of greater propulsion. Current
speed and drift displacement decreased with depth, consistent with the vertical
attenuation of TC-generated upper-ocean currents, whereas active propulsion
maintained mobility as the ambient flow weakened. At 2.0 m/s, active paths were
1.24--1.32 times longer than at 1.5 m/s, indicating that future speed upgrades
could expand subsurface sampling capacity subject to energy and control limits.

The present evidence remains simulation-based and uses a mission-level
horizontal point-mass model. The 40 and 60 m results are interim until their
training archives are complete; field validation, operational forecast impact,
and calibrated six-degree-of-freedom vehicle dynamics remain future work.
