Using the final tracking error as the operational observation benefit, the right
panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:surface_vmax_comparison} reports the primary
speed-limit comparison. Averaged over the four fleet sizes and ten training
seeds, the 20 m/s policy obtains final tracking errors of 197.1, 209.2, 260.9,
362.1, and 524.0 km at deployment radii of 100, 200, 400, 600, and 800 km,
respectively. The corresponding 12 m/s values are 221.0, 238.9, 317.5, 450.9,
and 635.1 km. Thus, 20 m/s yields a lower final tracking error in all 20
matched fleet-size/radius comparisons, corresponding to a higher operational
observation benefit. Its fleet-averaged advantage increases from 23.8 km at
100 km to 111.1 km at 800 km.

The absolute distances still increase with deployment radius for both speed
limits, which is expected because a finite propulsion speed cannot eliminate a
larger initial displacement within every segment duration. The objective is
therefore to minimize the final tracking error at each error level rather than to
make it independent of deployment error. The final-tracking-error advantage of
20 m/s is larger than the corresponding time-averaged advantage: 23.8 versus
10.1 km at 100 km and 111.1 versus 84.2 km at 800 km. This indicates that the
higher-speed policy continues correcting residual separation late in the
episode rather than merely remaining closer during the initial transit.

This benefit involves a clear propulsion trade-off. Relative to 20 m/s, the
12 m/s policy reduces path length per SUV by 78.7, 95.5, 147.4, 196.6, and
233.3 km at the five radii and lowers mean ground speed by 1.74--4.18 m/s.
Accordingly, 20 m/s provides the higher final observation benefit, whereas
12 m/s is preferable when shorter trajectories and lower propulsion use are
prioritized.

Time-averaged distance provides a secondary process-level diagnostic of
whether proximity is maintained throughout an episode. Across the twenty
matched fleet-size/radius comparisons, the 20 m/s policy has the smaller mean
time-averaged distance in all 20 cases. The median paired difference
$(d_{12}-d_{20})$ is +51.8 km. Uncorrected two-sided paired tests indicate
lower distance at 20 m/s in 19 cases and at 12 m/s in none of the cases at
$p<0.05$. Because these comparisons were not adjusted for multiplicity, they
provide exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence.

The relative time-averaged distance penalty of the 12 m/s policy is
+6.3\% at 100 km, +10.6\% at 200 km, +18.2\% at 400 km, +17.7\% at 600 km,
and +14.0\% at 800 km. The non-monotonic relative pattern, together with the
monotonic increase in absolute final-tracking-error benefit, is consistent with the
finite time available to remove forecast-induced displacement. Overall, the
results show that the policy learned from wind observations alone provides
increasing operational value for large initial displacement errors when a
20 m/s speed envelope is available, while the 12 m/s envelope offers a
lower-effort alternative.
